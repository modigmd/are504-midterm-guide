Topic 2 · Theory8 marks

1. Diagnose a damp wall before renovating

An occupied office has recurring damp staining at a wall–floor junction. The stain returns after repainting. As part of a Building Condition Assessment (BCA), explain:

  1. Which records and site evidence you would collect.
  2. Two plausible moisture sources or transport mechanisms, and how you would distinguish them.
  3. Two suitable investigation methods and one limitation of each.
  4. How you would prioritize action and verify a repair.
Topics 2–3 · Theory8 marks

2. Comfort and cooling-load boundaries

A facilities manager says, “The thermostat reads 24°C, so everyone must be comfortable. Also, air entering through cracks is the same thing as designed ventilation, so I can combine all cooling loads under envelope transmission.” Correct this reasoning.

Name the six main PMV inputs; explain what PMV = 0 and PPD mean; distinguish infiltration from ventilation and sensible from latent load; and state how envelope load relates to total building load.

Topic 4 · Theory8 marks

3. Choose the right thermal mechanism

A continuously cooled building has a heavy concrete roof, reflective foil pressed directly against its underside, insulation between conductive framing, and an exposed concrete slab extending through the facade.

Explain four issues: insulation versus thermal mass; the foil installation; heat bypassing cavity insulation; and how to improve the slab detail. State which heat-transfer mechanism each proposed correction addresses.

Topic 5 · Theory8 marks

4. An ordered renovation package for a coastal office

A hot-humid coastal office has large west-facing windows, uncontrolled leakage, afternoon overheating and high indoor humidity. Its owner proposes installing larger cooling equipment and photovoltaic panels immediately.

Recommend an ordered renovation package. Include a baseline assessment, at least three passive measures tied to specific loads, suitable active systems/controls, the role of renewables and a way to verify performance. Explain the limits of always-open natural ventilation in this case.

5. Climate evidence and passive airflow

You are comparing a hot-arid location with cool, dry nights and a hot-humid location with warm, humid nights.

  1. Compare the inputs, useful outputs and main limitations of Mahoney Tables and Climate Consultant.
  2. Explain the different airflow drivers of a windcatcher and a solar chimney.
  3. Explain where exposed thermal mass with night flushing may help, and why neither airflow device guarantees useful cooling or dehumidification.

Topics 3–4 · Calculation12 marks

7. Add insulation to an existing wall

An existing wall has total resistance 0.48 m²·K/W, including both surface films and all existing layers. Add a continuous 60 mm insulation layer with conductivity 0.036 W/(m·K). The net opaque wall area is 72 m² and the temperature difference is 16 K.

Assume steady one-dimensional heat transfer, no thermal bridges and unchanged surface films; ignore solar gain. Find the added layer's R, the new assembly U, the new wall heat-transfer rate and the percentage reduction from the original wall transmission. Use full precision until the final rounding.

Topic 3 · Calculation12 marks

8. Separate sensible and latent infiltration

A 360 m³ zone has uncontrolled infiltration of 0.65 air changes per hour. Outdoor/indoor temperatures are 35°C/23°C. Outdoor/indoor humidity ratios are 19/9 g of water per kg of dry air.

Use the lecture's simplified air approximation: density = 1.18 kg/m³, specific heat = 1.006 kJ/(kg·K), and latent heat = 2500 kJ/kg. Find airflow, sensible load, latent load and total infiltration load. Use humidity ratio in kg/kg in the latent equation. These are positive cooling loads; deliberately provided ventilation is excluded.

Topic 4 · Calculation12 marks

9. Derive ψ, then include all bridge terms

A detailed model of one junction gives 756 W. The matching plane-element reference gives 684 W. Both use a temperature difference of 18 K and a modeled junction length of 12 m, with the same dimensions and boundary conditions. The excess contains only this linear bridge; point bridges are excluded from this model. Derive its ψ-value.

Now apply that same ψ to a wall with net opaque area 48 m², plane-element U = 0.55 W/(m²·K), a total of 24 m of that junction, and six separate point fixings with χ = 0.08 W/K each. For a temperature difference of 18 K, calculate the full transmission coefficient H and heat-transfer rate. The stated plane-element U excludes these linear and point corrections; there are no other heat paths.

Topics 3 & 5 · Calculation14 marks

10. Complete envelope load and a shading improvement

At one cooling design condition, outdoor air is 41°C and indoor air is 24°C. A facade has gross area 150 m², including 18 m² of windows. Its opaque-wall U is 0.45 W/(m²·K); window U is 1.8 W/(m²·K). The roof area is 95 m² with U = 0.30 W/(m²·K).

The windows have SHGC = 0.32, incident irradiance 650 W/m² and an additional shading factor of 0.60. That shading factor is not already included in SHGC or irradiance. Infiltration contributes a given 1.05 kW sensible plus 1.32 kW latent. Ignore floor transfer, thermal bridges, opaque-surface solar/long-wave effects, deliberate ventilation and internal gains. Use steady heat transfer and count window conduction separately from solar gain.

Calculate transmission, window solar gain and total envelope load. Then external shading reduces only the shading factor to 0.35. Find the revised envelope load and the thermal cooling energy avoided if this same load reduction persists for 5 hours. Assume all other conditions remain constant. Report thermal energy, not HVAC electrical energy.
