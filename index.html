Topic 2 · Investigating existing conditions

Find the cause before the fix

A renovation starts with a measured baseline. Replacing equipment without diagnosing the building can leave the main problem untouched.

What a BCA tells you

A Building Condition Assessment evaluates physical condition, performance, defects and remaining useful life across the structure, envelope, HVAC, electrical, plumbing, life safety, interiors and site. Its output is a prioritized action plan supported by evidence.

A Property Condition Assessment is commonly a higher-level transaction and repair-cost review. A structural inspection concentrates on load-bearing elements. A BCA looks across the whole building.

  1. Prepare: define the purpose and scope; gather drawings, maintenance logs, previous reports and utility records; arrange safe access and staff interviews.
  2. Inspect and measure: walk the building systematically, photograph defects, record their locations and test suspected problems.
  3. Analyze: compare observations with records. Separate the visible symptom from the underlying cause.
  4. Report and act: rank urgency, recommend further tests or repairs, estimate remaining life and costs where supported, then verify the action.

Moisture: source → path → storage → damage

Rain, leaking plumbing, ground moisture or condensation supply water. Cracks, joints, capillaries and air leakage move it. Porous materials or concealed cavities retain it. The consequences include staining, mold, corrosion, decay and poorer thermal performance.

Example: painting over a damp patch treats the symptom. First determine whether rain is entering, a pipe is leaking, or humid air is condensing; then stop that source and repair the damaged assembly.

Condensation is a temperature threshold

Air has a dew point determined by its temperature and moisture content. Condensation can form when a surface is at or below that dew point:

Tsurface≤Tdew pointT_{\mathrm{surface}}\leq T_{\mathrm{dew\ point}}

When does the wall become wet?

Illustration at indoor air temperature 24°C. Change humidity and surface temperature.

Dew point uses a Magnus approximation for this illustration. Cold spots and humidity both matter. The threshold applies to the air touching the surface, including humid outdoor air reaching a cooled assembly.

Control it: correct water sources, maintain insulation continuity, seal unintended air paths, and provide controlled ventilation and dehumidification when needed. In a hot-humid climate, admitting more outdoor air can increase the moisture load.

Lecture shortcut to treat carefully

Topic 2 uses about 3 mm to contrast smaller and larger cracks. Use that as a lecture screening cue, together with crack pattern and movement; a small crack may still need investigation.

Structural patterns: continuous horizontal cracking, diagonal or stair-step cracks, and cracks that widen markedly toward one end merit attention to loading and movement. Uniform settlement moves the foundation down together; differential settlement moves parts by different amounts and can distort the structure; tipping produces rotation. Condition ratings and comfort

The lecture distinguishes a distressed-condition audit (score observed defects), an overall condition rating (judge the component as a whole) and a health index (combine condition information into an overall indicator).

Condition scoring: what the slide example means

The distress survey example multiplies a condition rating, CR, by a criticality factor, CF:

CS=CR×CFCS=CR\times CF

CR runs from 1 (very good) to 5 (very critical). Higher scores indicate worse condition or higher priority; for example, CR = 4 and CF = 5 give CS = 20, the severe band. The displayed score bands are: 1–5 no maintenance; 6–10 minor; 11–15 major; 16–20 severe; 21–25 very severe/immediate action. Major and severe bands require further investigation. These are this example’s bands, not a universal assessment scale.

The slide’s numerical defect-factor column corresponds to DF=1−CS/25DF=1-CS/25, so its direction is reversed: a higher DF indicates a better condition. The printed grouping is unclear; check the instructor’s convention if asked to reproduce the formula.

PMV predicts a group’s mean thermal sensation on a scale from −3 (cold), through 0 (neutral), to +3 (hot). PPD predicts the percentage dissatisfied. The six main inputs are air temperature, mean radiant temperature, air speed, relative humidity, metabolic activity (met) and clothing insulation (clo).

Topic 3 · Building envelope load

Turn the building into a heat balance

The envelope separates indoor and outdoor conditions. It controls heat, air and moisture; HVAC meets the remaining demand.

Q˙envelope=Q˙transmission+Q˙solar+Q˙infiltration\dot Q_{\mathrm{envelope}}=\dot Q_{\mathrm{transmission}}+\dot Q_{\mathrm{solar}}+\dot Q_{\mathrm{infiltration}}
Q˙building=Q˙envelope+Q˙ventilation+Q˙internal\dot Q_{\mathrm{building}}=\dot Q_{\mathrm{envelope}}+\dot Q_{\mathrm{ventilation}}+\dot Q_{\mathrm{internal}}

Infiltration is uncontrolled air leakage through cracks and gaps. Ventilation is deliberate outdoor-air supply for indoor air quality. People, lighting and equipment are internal loads; include them when assessing the total building, but do not call them envelope loads.

Sensible = temperature

Conduction, solar gain, lighting and ordinary electrical equipment mostly add sensible heat. People and outdoor air also have a sensible component.

Latent = moisture

Humid outdoor air, breathing, perspiration, cooking steam and evaporation add moisture that may need removal. People and outdoor air can create both load types.

Transmission: k → R → U → heat flow

QuantityMeaningSI units
k or λMaterial conductivity: how easily heat conductsW/(m·K)
R = L/kResistance of one layer, thickness L in metresm²·K/W
U = 1/RtotalTransmittance of the whole assemblyW/(m²·K)
Q̇ = UAΔTRate of transmission through area AW
Rtotal=Rsi+∑jLjkj+Rse,U=1RtotalR_{\mathrm{total}}=R_{si}+\sum_j\frac{L_j}{k_j}+R_{se},\qquad U=\frac{1}{R_{\mathrm{total}}}

Include internal/external surface resistances and relevant air-space resistance. Add layer resistances along a series path. For parallel paths, combine the conductances or use an appropriate whole-assembly method. More thickness or lower k raises R and lowers U.

The three heat-transfer mechanisms

Conduction moves heat through materials; convection exchanges it between a surface and moving air; radiation exchanges it between surfaces.

Q˙cond=−kAdTdx\dot Q_{\mathrm{cond}}=-kA\frac{dT}{dx}
Q˙conv=hA(Ts−Ta)\dot Q_{\mathrm{conv}}=hA(T_s-T_a)
Q˙rad=εσA(Ts4−Tsur4)\dot Q_{\mathrm{rad}}=\varepsilon\sigma A(T_s^4-T_{\mathrm{sur}}^4)

h is a surface heat-transfer coefficient in W/(m²·K), ε is emissivity, and σ ≈ 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W/(m²·K⁴). Use kelvin for fourth-power temperatures. The simplified radiation equation assumes suitable surrounding-surface conditions.

Windows: conduction and sunlight are different

Q˙window,cond=UwAwΔT\dot Q_{\mathrm{window,cond}}=U_wA_w\Delta T
Q˙window,solar=Aw SHGC I Fsh\dot Q_{\mathrm{window,solar}}=A_w\,SHGC\,I\,F_{sh}

SHGC is the fraction of incident solar energy that becomes indoor heat gain. I is incident irradiance in W/m²; Fsh is the remaining solar fraction after shading (1 = no shading). Lower SHGC or better shading reduces solar gain; lower U reduces temperature-driven transfer. Visible transmittance describes daylight, a different property.

Opaque surfaces: sun changes the effective outdoor temperature

Tsa≈Tout+αI−εΔRhoT_{sa}\approx T_{out}+\frac{\alpha I-\varepsilon\Delta R}{h_o}
Q˙opaque≈UA(Tsa−Tin)\dot Q_{\mathrm{opaque}}\approx UA(T_{sa}-T_{in})

α is solar absorptance; ho is the exterior surface coefficient; ΔR is a net long-wave radiation correction. Dark, high-absorptance roofs usually gain more sun heat. Reflective finishes reduce absorption. The lecture gives ΔR=σ(Tout4−Tsky4)\Delta R=\sigma(T_{out}^4-T_{sky}^4), with temperatures in kelvin.

Avoid double counting

UA(Tsa − Tin) already includes the outdoor-air temperature effect and an opaque-surface solar/radiation correction. Use it as that surface’s combined load; do not add another full UAΔT for the same surface. Window conduction and window solar gain remain separate terms.

Infiltration: air carries heat and water

V˙=ACH×V3600\dot V=\frac{ACH\times V}{3600}

ACH is air changes per hour (h⁻¹); V is the room volume (m³); airflow V̇ is m³/s. Then:

Q˙s=ρcpV˙(Tout−Tin)\dot Q_s=\rho c_p\dot V(T_{out}-T_{in})
Q˙l≈ρV˙hfg(Wout−Win)\dot Q_l\approx\rho\dot Vh_{fg}(W_{out}-W_{in})

For these lecture approximations, use ρ ≈ 1.2 kg/m³, cp ≈ 1.006 kJ/(kg·K) and hfg ≈ 2500 kJ/kg. These give loads in kW. W is humidity ratio in kg water/kg dry air; values given in g/kg must be divided by 1000.

Topic 4 · Insulation and thermal bridges

Continuity matters as much as thickness

A well-insulated wall can still perform poorly if heat bypasses the insulation at slabs, corners, frames or fixings.

Choose the assembly, not just a material

Insulation slows heat transfer. Thermal mass stores heat and delays temperature change. They can work together, but mass does not replace thermal resistance. In a continuously air-conditioned hot-climate building, first limit unwanted heat entering the building; mass is useful only when its charging and heat-release cycle helps the operating strategy.

ConfigurationMain advantageMain design check
Exterior continuous insulationCovers structure and preserves usable indoor areaKeep continuity at openings, slabs, roof and foundations; coordinate cladding and fire/moisture details.
Cavity insulationFits within existing framing or masonry cavitiesFraming and incomplete fill can bypass the insulation.
Interior insulationUseful where the façade must remain unchangedLoss of floor area, disruption, junction bridges and moisture risk.
Sandwich/SIP/ICF systemsIntegrates insulation with the construction systemConnections and penetrations still need detailing.
Radiant barrierA low-emissivity surface reduces radiation across an adjacent air spaceNeeds an air gap and a suitable clean reflective surface; conventional insulation is still needed to resist conduction.

Materials include mineral/glass fibres, EPS, polyurethane, loose fill, spray foams, bio-based products and vacuum insulation panels (VIPs). For equal R, thickness is proportional to k: L=RkL=Rk. Using the slide’s illustrative k-values, R = 2 m²·K/W needs about 80 mm glass fibre (0.040), 76 mm EPS (0.038), 44 mm PU (0.022), or 12 mm VIP (0.006).

These are lecture comparison values, not product specifications. A VIP still has nonzero effective conductivity; edge losses, ageing, damage and installation affect its performance. A radiant barrier’s whole-building savings are not a fixed percentage.

Saudi roof discussion: combine a suitable solar-reflective roof surface, continuous bulk insulation, coordinated waterproofing/air/moisture control, and radiant control where its installation is appropriate. The lecture’s foil example uses an adjacent air space of at least 2.5 cm; follow the actual system’s detail. Foil pressed against a solid layer does not provide the intended radiant-barrier action across an air gap.

Thermal diffusivity: how quickly a temperature change spreads
αth=kρcp[m2/s]\alpha_{\mathrm{th}}=\frac{k}{\rho c_p}\quad[\mathrm{m^2/s}]

Lower diffusivity means a temperature disturbance propagates more slowly. Greater volumetric heat capacity, ρcp, means more heat storage per unit volume and temperature rise. Thermal mass can reduce the swing and delay the peak; neither effect by itself proves reduced daily cooling energy.

Thermal versus acoustic properties

Thermal performance uses k, R and U. Sound absorption reduces reflections within a room; absorption coefficient α and NRC describe this behavior. Sound insulation reduces transmission between spaces; transmission loss and STC describe it. A good absorber is not automatically a good sound barrier. Sealing gaps, mass and assembly connections matter.

The lecture’s impedance (Kundt’s) tube tests small samples’ acoustic behavior. Its example ranges are about 100–1600 Hz for a 10 cm sample and 500–5000 Hz for a 3 cm sample; these ranges depend on the apparatus.

Recognize the bridge

A thermal bridge is a localized region with greater heat transfer because of geometry, conductive materials or a discontinuity in insulation. It adds load, changes surface temperatures and can contribute to discomfort, condensation and durability problems.

  • Repeating: studs or regularly spaced structural members.
  • Non-repeating: slab edges, roof/wall junctions, corners, window perimeters or balcony connections.
  • Random: gaps, voids or isolated conductive penetrations.

These occurrence categories are separate from dimensional form: an extended junction is modeled with linear transmittance ψ; an isolated penetration may use point transmittance χ. A recurring fixing can still be a point bridge.

Worked calculation · corrected transmission

Surface term: U = 0.40 W/(m²·K), A = 100 m². One junction: ψ = 0.20 W/(m·K), L = 20 m. Ten point fixings: χ = 0.05 W/K each. ΔT = 15 K.

H=(0.40)(100)+(0.20)(20)+10(0.05)=44.5 W/KH=(0.40)(100)+(0.20)(20)+10(0.05)=44.5\ \mathrm{W/K}
Q˙=44.5(15)=667.5 W\dot Q=44.5(15)=667.5\ \mathrm{W}

Ignoring the bridges gives 600 W, underestimating by 67.5 W. The bridge addition is 11.25% of the uncorrected surface load; its share of the corrected total is about 10.1%. Check what denominator a question asks for.

Do not count a bridge twice. If a whole-wall U-value already incorporates the repeating framing effect, do not add that same effect again as a separate correction. Keep surface areas, reference dimensions and ψ conventions consistent.

Derive ψ by subtracting the idealized surface flow

ψ=Q˙with bridge−Q˙surface onlyLΔT\psi=\frac{\dot Q_{\mathrm{with\ bridge}}-\dot Q_{\mathrm{surface\ only}}}{L\Delta T}

A 2D/3D model calculates the real junction flow. Subtract the U×A reference contributions, then divide by junction length and temperature difference. The lecture links this detailed calculation to ISO 10211; ISO 14683 covers simplified methods and default ψ-values.

Simulation and identification: what to remember

Define geometry and materials, set indoor/outdoor boundary conditions, mesh the junction, solve the heat flow, then check the result and surface temperatures. THERM, HEAT2/HEAT3 and similar tools are examples from the lecture. Model boundaries must be sufficiently far from the junction and the mesh sufficiently refined for the solution to converge.

Topic 4 gives a classroom modeling cue of at least max(3 × component thickness, 1 m) on either side and mentions a 25 mm mesh. Treat these as lecture guidance, not a complete statement of the standard or a guarantee of accuracy.

On site, IRT locates temperature anomalies. A drone or a registered 3D model can extend the survey, but a thermal image alone does not establish ψ. Drawings, site observations and a consistent calculation model complete the diagnosis.

What makes a high-performance renovation?

Sustainability includes energy, water, materials, life-cycle impact and circularity. High performance also requires measured comfort, indoor air quality, durability and good operation. A certificate and actual measured performance answer different questions.

Topic 5 · Passive and active strategies

Choose measures that work together

Reduce the need for heating and cooling, meet the remaining demand efficiently, then integrate renewables and verify actual performance.

Passive measures reduce the demand

Shading, insulation, airtightness, orientation, appropriate glazing, thermal mass and favorable-condition natural ventilation use the building’s form and physical behavior.

Active systems meet and control it

HVAC, mechanical ventilation, humidity control, efficient lighting, sensors and controls use equipment. Renewables such as PV supply energy; they do not remove the need to reduce the load.

A passive design can use a controlled damper or motorized shade. Classify the primary function: shading still reduces solar demand, while its automation is an active control.

Let the climate set the priorities

ClimatePrioritiesWatch out for
Hot-arid · Riyadh comparisonSolar control, insulation, airtightness; consider exposed thermal mass and night purge when nights are sufficiently cool and clean.Hot daytime air, dust, and mass that cannot release stored heat. Evaporative cooling uses water and adds moisture.
Hot-humid · Dhahran comparisonSolar control, a tight envelope, controlled fresh air, efficient cooling and reliable dehumidification.Warm humid nights and salt/dust exposure. Uncontrolled natural ventilation may add a large latent load.

These are the lecture’s broad climate comparisons. Check actual weather, occupancy, outdoor air quality and operating periods before choosing a strategy.

Know what each passive device actually does

Three devices, three mechanisms

Conceptual sections, not construction details. Natural ventilation exchanges air; it does not dehumidify the incoming air.

  • Windcatcher: captures wind and uses pressure differences to move air. Suitable wind and favorable outdoor conditions are essential; dampers and filtration help control dust and unwanted air.
  • Solar chimney: sunlight warms air in a shaft; buoyancy exhausts it upward and draws replacement air through openings. Useful with strong sun even when wind is weak. Hot/humid replacement air can still increase cooling demand.
  • Trombe wall: glazing and an air gap sit in front of dark thermal mass. The wall absorbs solar heat and releases it later, primarily for heating. Summer shading and vent arrangements prevent overheating; a summer exhaust mode can operate like a solar chimney.
  • Water jars / evaporative cooling: evaporation takes sensible heat from the air and adds water vapour. More useful in dry air; limited in humid air.

Shading is a load-control decision

Fixed external overhangs, fins and screens block direct sun; adjustable louvers adapt to it. Interior blinds mainly control glare and daylight, while between-glass systems protect the device from weather. Trees, pergolas and Mashrabiya add architectural or seasonal shading. Choose orientation and sun angles carefully: low-angle east/west sun is harder to block with a simple horizontal overhang.

Mass needs a way to discharge

Thermal mass helps when solar gains are controlled, the mass is exposed to indoor air, and cool night air can remove the stored heat. In a warm, humid night, that discharge route may be ineffective or add moisture. Insulation limits transfer; mass shifts timing. More mass or more insulation is not automatically the best complete design.

Study workflow: obtain a representative weather file; use Mahoney to form the first hypothesis; use hourly charts to check when the strategy is suitable; then test important combined measures with simulation and appropriate details. Read a psychrometric chart’s horizontal axis as dry-bulb temperature and vertical axis as humidity ratio, with curved relative-humidity lines.

For a recommendation, state climate evidence + operating period + building measure + verification method. For example: “During sufficiently cool, dry, low-dust nights, enable a controlled purge to discharge exposed mass; verify indoor temperature, humidity and next-day cooling demand.”

Build a coherent renovation sequence

  1. Baseline: identify envelope defects, comfort/IAQ complaints, energy use and system condition.
  2. Objectives: set measurable targets, such as reduced cooling energy while maintaining specified indoor conditions.
  3. Passive package: correct moisture, add appropriate continuous insulation, improve shading/glazing and seal uncontrolled leakage.
  4. Active package: size HVAC for the revised load; select efficient equipment and effective humidity/ventilation controls. Coordinate lighting, sensors and schedules.
  5. Renewables and verification: assess suitable supply options, commission the systems, meter performance, and compare before/after under comparable weather and use.

Why sequence matters: envelope improvements change peak demand. Sizing new HVAC before accounting for them can produce oversized equipment and poor part-load or humidity performance. Simple addition of separate measures’ predicted savings can also overstate a combined package’s savings because the measures interact.

Peak demand versus annual energy: peak load is the largest required heat rate in kW; annual energy is accumulated over operating hours in kWh/year. Shifting a heat peak with thermal mass can help capacity or timing without necessarily reducing annual energy. Verify both quantities when they matter.

What to say about efficient active systems

Use efficient HVAC, appropriate capacity, good distribution, controls and maintenance; provide deliberate fresh air and humidity control. Heat/energy recovery may reduce outdoor-air conditioning demand when appropriate to climate and operation. Efficient lighting reduces electrical use and sensible internal gains; daylight controls must balance glare and solar heat. Commissioning checks that installed equipment and controls perform as intended.

A useful supplemental definition is COP=Q˙useful/PelectricCOP=\dot Q_{\mathrm{useful}}/P_{\mathrm{electric}}. For a cooling system, a higher COP means more cooling per unit electrical input under the stated conditions. Compare equipment at equivalent conditions and part-load requirements.

Can you explain it without the notes?

Original practice questions based on the lecture topics. These are not past exam questions.

1. A room has mold at a cold corner. What should you investigate?

Check moisture sources, local surface temperature, indoor humidity, air leakage, water entry, insulation continuity and ventilation operation. Compare the surface temperature with the dew point. Choose thermography and moisture measurements to support the visual survey. Determine the source/path before specifying repair; a thermal bridge and a moisture source can act together.

2. A wall is 120 m² including 20 m² of windows. U = 0.6, ΔT = 18 K. What is its opaque transmission?

Net opaque area = 100 m². Q˙=0.6(100)(18)=1080 W=1.08 kW\dot Q=0.6(100)(18)=1080\ \mathrm W=1.08\ \mathrm{kW}. Calculate window conduction and solar gain separately.

3. What resistance does 80 mm of insulation at k = 0.04 provide?

R=0.080/0.04=2.0 m2K/WR=0.080/0.04=2.0\ \mathrm{m^2 K/W}. This is the insulation layer’s resistance. To calculate assembly U, add the other layer and surface resistances first.

4. A 5 m² window has SHGC 0.3, I = 800 W/m² and shading factor 0.4. Find solar gain.

Q˙=5(0.3)(800)(0.4)=480 W\dot Q=5(0.3)(800)(0.4)=480\ \mathrm W. Its U-value is needed for conduction, not for this solar term.

5. A 200 m³ zone has 0.9 ACH. Find airflow and sensible load for ΔT = 10 K.

V˙=0.9(200)/3600=0.05 m3/s\dot V=0.9(200)/3600=0.05\ \mathrm{m^3/s}. Then Q˙s=1.2(1.006)(0.05)(10)=0.604 kW\dot Q_s=1.2(1.006)(0.05)(10)=0.604\ \mathrm{kW}. You need a humidity-ratio difference to find its latent component.

6. A junction model gives 900 W; surface-only flow is 840 W. L = 10 m and ΔT = 20 K. Find ψ.

ψ=(900−840)/(10×20)=0.30 W/(mK)\psi=(900-840)/(10\times20)=0.30\ \mathrm{W/(m K)}. Check that the model and surface reference use matching boundary conditions and dimensions.

7. Why can opening windows in Dhahran increase cooling demand?

Outdoor air may be hotter and more humid. It adds sensible and latent loads, even if airflow feels pleasant. Operate natural ventilation only in favorable temperature, humidity and air-quality periods; otherwise use controlled ventilation and appropriate cooling/dehumidification.

8. A well-insulated wall has an exposed balcony slab. Is adding more wall insulation enough?

The slab may bypass the wall insulation. Detail a suitable structural thermal break aligned with the insulation and coordinate waterproofing/fire requirements. Quantify the remaining ψ contribution; increasing center-of-wall R alone leaves the bridge path.

9. Does a solar chimney cool or dehumidify any incoming air?

It creates buoyancy-driven extraction. Replacement air still has outdoor conditions unless separately treated; the chimney does not inherently dehumidify or refrigerate it.

10. What does PMV = 0 say, and why is a questionnaire useful?

It predicts neutral average thermal sensation for the modeled group and conditions. PPD remains about 5%; real preferences vary. A questionnaire checks actual occupant experience, while measurements explain the environmental cause.

Common mistakes to catch

  • Using k as if it were U, or calculating resistance as k/L.
  • Leaving thickness in mm or humidity ratio in g/kg.
  • Using 60 instead of 0.60 for 60% RH.
  • Counting windows as opaque wall area.
  • Multiplying a kW result by 1000 twice, or treating kW as kWh.
  • Forgetting that ΔT multiplies surface, linear and point conductances.
  • Adding the same solar or thermal-bridge effect twice.
  • Calling a solar chimney a dehumidifier, or thermal mass insulation.
  • Claiming a component’s load reduction equals total energy savings.
